**The genome sequence of *Aspergillus oryzae*, a fungus used in the production of the traditional Japanese fermentation foods *sake* (rice wine), *shoyu* (soy sauce), and *miso* (soybean paste), has revealed prominent features in its gene composition as compared to those of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* and *Neurospora crassa*. The *A. oryzae* genome is extremely enriched with genes involved in biomass degradation, primary and secondary metabolism, transcriptional regulation, and cell signaling. Even compared to the related species *A. nidulans* and *A. fumigatus*, an abundance of metabolic genes is apparent, with acquisition of more than 6 Mb of sequence in the *A. oryzae* lineage, interspersed throughout the *A. oryzae* genome. Besides the various already established merits of *A. oryzae* for industrial uses, the genome sequence and the abundance of metabolic genes should significantly accelerate the biotechnological use of *A. oryzae* in industry.**

**Key words:** genome sequence; *Aspergillus oryzae*; metabolic genes

After the recognition of DNA structure in 1953, progress in the methodology of molecular biology, biotechnology, and computer biology within a span of 50 years has enabled researchers to rapidly determine complete genome sequences of living organisms. Several model organisms have already revealed their whole genetic information to the public, and genome research has entered a second stage, in which the genome sequences of many organisms of interest have been determined or are being determined, the aim being to understand the specific phenotypic characteristics of each organism by comparative genomics.

Filamentous fungi include a number of species that greatly affect human life. Some are extensively used in industry to produce fermented foods, industrial enzymes, and secondary metabolites, while others cause damage to plants and animals. A model organism, *Neurospora crassa*, is the first filamentous fungus whose genome sequence has been published.<sup>1)</sup> But prior to the report on the *N. crassa* genome, a number of genome sequencing projects of filamentous fungi were launched. Currently, in addition to the genomes of two model fungi, *N. crassa* and *Aspergillus nidulans*, various fungal genomes have been or are being completely sequenced. These include industrially important *A. oryzae* and *A. niger*, the aflatoxin producer *A. flavus*, the plant pathogens *Magnaporthe grisea*, *Fusarium graminearum*, and *Ustilago maydis*, and the animal pathogens *A. fumigatus*, *Cryptococcus neoformans*, and *Coccidioides immitis*. Five genomes in the genus *Aspergillus* have been determined, probably because they are so closely involved in human activity, positively as well as negatively. Consequently, *Aspergillus* is now the best genus to clarify by comparative genome analysis the fundamental reasons why some have beneficial characteristics for humans while others are harmful.

In this fermentation, *A. oryzae* secretes significant amounts of amylases and/or proteases to break down complex starches to sugars and proteins to peptide/amino acids, which are further fermented by yeast and lactic acid bacteria. This long history of extensive use in the food industry placed *A. oryzae* on the list of Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) organisms by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the United States of America. # I. Basic Features of the A. oryzae Genome

## 1. Overview of A. oryzae genome sequencing

Aspergillus oryzae RIB40 (National Research Institute Culture Stock, ATCC42149) is the strain of which the genome sequence has been determined.<sup>2)</sup> This strain shares typical characteristics in terms of morphology, growth, and amylase production with industrial strains used for sake brewing, and also has the potential to produce strong protease activity, which is important in soy sauce fermentation. Although neither a genetic nor a physical map was available when genome sequencing was launched, the genes and DNA sequences of RIB40 are the best characterized of the *A. oryzae* strains, and a large number of ESTs have been accumulated.<sup>3)</sup> The EST database covers not only the genes expressed in laboratory liquid culture under various growth conditions, but also those in solid state culture that has been extensively used in Japanese industry in sake brewing, soy sauce fermentation, and industrial enzyme production.

The genome sequencing of *A. oryzae* was accomplished by the whole genome shotgun (WGS) approach. High quality sequence reads of over 500,000 WGS clones by arrayed capillary DNA sequencers reached 7X depth of coverage of the whole genome. Approximately 20,000 cosmid and 9,000 BAC clones were used for connection of the WGS contigs. Consequently, all the raw sequences were assembled into contigs of 36.9 Mb

in total, with approximately 9X depth of coverage. Greater than 99.15% of the assembly was supported by more than two independent BAC or cosmid clones. Chromosome assignment of the contigs and their relative positioning in each chromosome were based on Southern hybridization against the chromosomes separated by PFGE (pulse field gel electrophoresis) and by the fingerprinting method.<sup>4)</sup> Finally, the entire assembly, including the mapping of contigs, was validated by the optical mapping.<sup>2)</sup>

A combination of several gene calling programs was employed to predict genes in the *A. oryzae* genome. A BLASTX homology search followed by homology-based gene structure prediction with ALN<sup>5)</sup> was used to identify genes encoding homologs of the proteins in the public databases. Novel genes were predicted by the *ab initio* gene finders GeneDecoder<sup>6)</sup> and GlimmerM<sup>7)</sup> using 489 genes predicted by ALN with high reliability as a learning set. The predicted genes were validated against *A. oryzae* ESTs (unpublished data) and *A. flavus* ESTs<sup>8)</sup> by alignment using SIM4.<sup>9)</sup> All the predicted protein sequences were annotated by searching against the COG<sup>10)</sup> database using BLASTP, followed by manual correction. Identification of transfer RNAs was based on tRNAScan-SE,<sup>11)</sup> and repeated sequences were detected using RepeatMasker.

A fully annotated version of the *A. oryzae* genome and those of *A. nidulans* and *A. fumigatus* are available at the websites http://www.bio.nite.go.jp/dogan/Top, http://www.broad.mit.edu/annotation/fungi/aspergillus/, and http://www.tigr.org/tdb/e2k1/afu1/ respectively.<sup>2,12,13)</sup>

## 2. Overall structure of the A. oryzae genome

The *A. oryzae* genome consists of eight chromosomes numbered I–VIII in decreasing order of size, with nucleotide lengths of 6.3 Mb, 6.2 Mb, 5.0 Mb, 4.8 Mb, 4.4 Mb, 4.1 Mb, 3.4 Mb (including 0.7 Mb rDNA repeats), and 3.3 Mb, resulting in a total genome size of 37.6 Mb (Fig. 1).<sup>2)</sup> The mitochondrial DNA is 28.9 kb in size. In accordance with the existence of eight chromosomes, 16 DNA fragments containing telomeric repeats (TTAGGGTCAACA) have been identified.<sup>14)</sup> It should be noted that this chromosome numbering is different from the one previously reported.<sup>15)</sup> The longest DNA fragment on PFGE, previously assigned to chromosome I, consists of chromosomes I and II, while the shortest fragment, previously shown to contain two chromosomes (chromosomes VII and VIII), consists a single chromosome, chromosome VIII. Centromeric sequences have not yet been obtained due to unsuccessful cloning of the centromeric fragments, probably due to the extremely high AT-content and/or DNA curvature.<sup>16)</sup> The *A. oryzae* genome size is very close to those of *A. niger*<sup>17)</sup> and *A. flavus* (J. Yu, personal communication), and 20–30% larger than those of *A. nidulans* and *A. fumigatus*.<sup>12,13)</sup>

## 3. Distinctive features of *A. oryzae* genome

Although *A. oryzae* and *S. cerevisiae* are closely related to each other in the phylogenetic tree, *A. oryzae* has an approximately 2.5-times bigger genome size and approximately twice as many genes as *S. cerevisiae*.<sup>18,19)</sup> Figure 2 shows a comparison of the number of genes in each COG functional category, and aims to address differences in cellular function between the two organisms. As is clear, most of the genes expanded in the *A. oryzae* genome as compared to *S. cerevisiae* pertain

to metabolism (C, G, E, F, H, I, P, and Q), among which Q (secondary metabolism) genes are the most significant. Expansion of the functionally unknown genes (R, S, and X, general function prediction only, function unknown and no homology to any COGs respectively) is also significant to the same extent. In addition, some expansion is evident in the Y (nuclear structure), V (defense mechanisms), T (signal transduction mechanisms), and M (cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis) genes.<sup>2)</sup> Considering the distribution of the absolute number of genes in each COG category (Fig. 2A), the expansion of metabolic genes contributes most significantly to the expansion of the total gene number in *A. oryzae* as compared to *S. cerevisiae*. Searches for orthologs conserved between *A. oryzae* and *A. fumigatus* and *A. oryzae* and *A. nidulans* have revealed that the expansion of metabolic genes in *A. oryzae* occurs specifically in several particular metabolic pathways.<sup>2)</sup> The most highly expanded metabolic genes are for phenylalanine/tryptophan degradation, toluene/m-cresol/p-cymene degradation, and biosynthesis/degradation of hydrophobic amino acids. Pyruvate decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase genes involved in phenylalanine/tryptophan degradation are known to be the most highly expressed genes in the presence of glucose.<sup>21)</sup> In fermentation industry, *A. oryzae* is grown on the surface of steamed rice and steamed ground soybean, which are deficient in nitrogen sources without proteolytic digestion followed by amino acid degradation. Hence the genes for amino acid catabolism, in addition to the protease genes described below, might be expanded to ensure effective utilization of external nitrogen sources in the raw materials.

The genomes of three *Aspergillus* species *A. oryzae*, *A. fumigatus* and *A. nidulans* share overall conserved synteny with each other.<sup>2,12,13)</sup> However, as compared to *A. fumigatus* and *A. nidulans*, strikingly more synteny breaks are present in the *A. oryzae* genome. In other words, the *A. oryzae* genome has a mosaic structure consisting of loci common to all three *Aspergillus* species, and some specific to *A. oryzae*. Since no syntenic regions have been observed inside the *A. oryzae* genome, the *A. oryzae*-specific DNA segments are thought to emerge by horizontal transfer from foreign organisms rather than by gene duplication.<sup>2)</sup> Interestingly, genes related to secondary metabolism and genes with strong similarity to bacterial ones are highly enriched in the *A. oryzae*-specific loci. Mapping of ESTs<sup>2)</sup> and preliminary DNA microarray experiments (unpublished data) have revealed that most of these genes are not expressed under ordinary growth conditions.

# II. Hydrolytic Enzymes

## 1. Glycolytic enzymes

Filamentous fungi belonging to the genus *Aspergillus*, in particular *A. oryzae* and *A. niger* (*A. awamori*), have been used in the production of copious amounts of polysaccharide-degrading enzymes, including amylolytic enzymes. These capabilities allow them to be commonly employed in the production of alcoholic beverages (viz., sake and shochu) in Japan for more than a thousand years. To date, a number of amylolytic genes have been cloned and characterized from *A. oryzae* due to their important role in the sake manufacturing industry. In

sake fermentation, three types of amylolytic enzymes are involved in the degradation of starch in rice grains. $\alpha$-Amylase (Taka-amylase A) hydrolyzes starch into dextrin and smaller oligosaccharides, from which glucose is then released by glucoamylase and $\alpha$-glucosidase. *A. oryzae* generally has multiple genes encoding Taka-amylase A, the number of which is two or three, depending on the strain.<sup>22)</sup> In addition, two kinds of glucoamylase-encoding genes have so far been cloned from *A. oryzae*; one gene (*glaA*)<sup>23)</sup> is highly homologous to the *A. niger* (*A. awamori*) *glaA* gene<sup>24,25)</sup> and is expressed both in submerged and solid-state culture, and the other (*glaB*)<sup>26)</sup> is also similar to the *A. niger glaA*, except for the absence of a starch-binding domain in the C-terminus, and the fact that it is expressed specifically in solid-state culture. One of three Taka-amylase genes (*amyA*) and the $\alpha$-glucosidase-encoding gene (*agdA*)<sup>27)</sup> are clustered with their transcriptional activator gene (*amyR*) in the *A. oryzae* genome.<sup>28)</sup>

Genome analysis of *A. oryzae* has predicted that, in addition to the amylolytic genes stated above, there exist two additional genes encoding $\alpha$-amylase (AO090120000263 and AO090003001498), and four genes encoding extracellular $\alpha$-glucosidase (AO090701000558, AO090102000559, AO090005001084, and AO090038000471). The AO090038000471 is an ortholog to the *A. nidulans agdB* gene coding for $\alpha$-glucosidase, with high transglycosylation activity.<sup>29)</sup> Overall, there are fewer amylolytic genes in *A. oryzae* than expected; however, the existence of multiple functional Taka-amylase genes that can confer high starch-degrading activity would be sufficient to make *A. oryzae* a major fungus for sake manufacturing in Japan.

Besides amylolytic genes, as compared with *Neurospora crassa*, whose genome analysis has been done,<sup>1)</sup> *A. oryzae* has more genes encoding glycosyl hydrolases. For example, it possesses more than 20 $\beta$-glucosidases and xylosidases assigned to family 3 of glycosyl hydrolase (http://afmb.cnrs-mrs.fr/CAZY/), a number twice that for *N. crassa* (Fig. 3). Notably, whereas *N. crassa* has a small number of pectinolytic genes, viz., 1 pectin methylesterase, 2 polygalacturonases, and 2 pectin/pectate lyases, *A. oryzae* has a number of such genes, viz., 5 pectin methylesterases, 19 polygalacturonases, and 15 pectin/pectate lyases (Figs. 4, 5). *A. niger* is known to be a good producer of pectinolytic enzymes, for most of which the corresponding genes have been isolated from *A. niger*.<sup>30)</sup> Furthermore, the expression profiles of 26 pectinolytic genes identified have been investigated in *A. niger*.<sup>31)</sup> Pectinolytic enzymes of *A. oryzae* play an important role in soy sauce fermentation, in which degradation of soybean pectin leads to efficient solubilization of soybean protein and to improved press-filtration of the fermented soy sauce mash. In contrast, another noteworthy feature of *A. oryzae* hydrolytic genes is an apparent decrease in the number of genes with a cellulose-binding domain (CBD) or a starch-binding domain (SBD). Fungal cellulases often contain a CBD in either the N- or the C-terminal region of the protein, and this has been shown to increase cellulolytic activity toward insoluble cellulose.<sup>32)</sup> Also, some amylolytic enzymes, such as $\alpha$-amylase and glucoamylase, contain an SBD, which allows an amylase to bind and efficiently digest raw, granular starch.<sup>33)</sup> Whereas *N. crassa* has 19 genes with a CBD, *A. oryzae* has only five CBD-containing genes, among which one gene, coding for a protein with similarity to $\beta$-mannanase of *A. aculeatus*, contains a CBD at the N-terminus. Most enzymes isolated so far from Aspergilli have a CBD in the C-terminal region of the protein, but in the *A. oryzae* genome there exists a gene encoding a protein with a CBD at the N-terminus, which suggests that hydrolytic enzymes with a CBD at the N-terminus commonly exist in other Aspergilli. On the other hand, the black Aspergilli *A. niger* and *A. kawachii* have been found to have an $\alpha$-amylase with an SBD, and also to have at least one glucoamylase with an SBD, but only

one glucoamylase gene (*glaA*) exists in *A. oryzae* as an SBD-containing amylase. The $\alpha$-amylase with an SBD in the black Aspergilli is an acid-stable $\alpha$-amylase, which is highly homologous to Taka-amylase A in its catalytic domain, and can digest raw starch efficiently.<sup>34)</sup> In contrast to the black Aspergilli, no gene coding for $\alpha$-amylase with an SBD has been found in *A. oryzae*. Conversely, to date, other Aspergilli have no ortholog of *A. oryzae glaB*, encoding glucoamylase without an SBD, which is specifically expressed when grown on steamed rice grains.<sup>26)</sup> Taken together, comparative study appears to support the hypothesis<sup>35)</sup> that *A. oryzae* is a domesticated fungus derived evolutionarily from a wild plant pathogenic species, *A. flavus*, although *A. oryzae* is now considered to be non-pathogenic. Furthermore, *A. oryzae* has perhaps lost cellulolytic and amylolytic enzymes with a substrate-binding domain during domestication, because it is not necessary for the fungus to digest insoluble cellulose or raw starch in the fermented food production process.

Half of the metalloproteinases and most of the aspartic proteinases and the serine-type carboxypeptidases have a typical signal sequence, suggesting that these enzymes are secreted enzymes. As to intracellular proteinases, there are four insulinase genes, seven ubiquitin-specific protease genes, and six ATP dependent proteinase genes. None of these genes has a homolog in the *A. oryzae* genome, suggesting that each of them has a distinctive physiological role.

Three pairs of highly homologous genes are present in each of the serine-type carboxypeptidases, metalloendopeptidases, and aspartic proteinases, and most of the pairs share the same number of introns. As an example, the phylogenetic tree of the aspartic endopeptidases is shown (Fig. 6). The genes in each pair (A, B, and C) have homology of 60–86%, and they have the identical number of introns at identical positions. However, none of them appears to be a pseudogene, since they are dispersed in the *A. oryzae* genome and they possess completely conserved active site motifs. The highly homologous pairs must have emerged by relatively recent gene duplication. The reason *A. oryzae* has so many secreted peptidase genes might also be explained by gene duplication occurred in the distant past. In any case, the abundance of secreted peptidases suggests that *A. oryzae* must have been selected as a high producer of secreted peptidases for fermentation of proteinous materials such as soybean.

# III. Transcription Factors

### 1. Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> transcription factors

Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> binuclear cluster DNA-binding proteins are transcriptional activators specific to fungi.<sup>43)</sup> Most of them bind to an inverted, everted, or direct repeat of CGG separated by an unconserved short spacing sequence. Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors usually act as pathway-specific activators that respond to a small molecular weight inducer to coordinate expression of structural genes of related function. Genes under the control of the Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors include those for utilization of carbon and nitrogen sources, and those for biosynthesis of primary and secondary metabolites. The prototype of Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors is Gal4p of *S. cerevisiae*, which activates transcription of galactose utilization genes in response to galactose.<sup>44)</sup> As is to be expected from the variety of utilizable carbon and nitrogen sources in nature and of primary and secondary metabolites produced, Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors constitute the largest family among the transcription factors found in fungi.

Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors must possess at least three functional domains, *viz.* DNA binding, transcriptional activation, and signal sensing domains, to perform signal-dependent transcriptional activation. To date, no conserved motif has been established for the activation domain in this family, and the signal-sensing domain must be unconserved since each factor responds to a different signal. The DNA binding domain contains the Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> motif, which has been used to identify transcription factors in this family in the *A. oryzae* genome. In addition, the Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors have a relatively well-conserved domain called a middle homology domain of unknown function. Therefore, proteins with the middle homology domain and/or with significant homology to the Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors in other organisms have been examined manually.

*S. cerevisiae* possesses 53 genes encoding the proteins of this family, while *N. crassa* has 77 open reading frames with the Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> motif.<sup>45)</sup> Compared to these organisms, *A. oryzae* possesses many more Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors, 187. Since the number of proteins with the Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> motif is also high in *A. nidulans*, expansion of Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors might be characteristic of the genus *Aspergillus*. Considering that most of these factors characterized to date are involved in the regulation of metabolic genes, the expansion of the Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors appears consistent with the expansion of metabolic genes described above. Another noteworthy observation on this family is that over 30% of *A. oryzae* Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> genes have no obvious orthologs in *A. nidulans*. These factors unique to *A. oryzae* might control expression of *A. oryzae*-specific genes.

Among the genes coding for *A. oryzae* Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors, only 39 genes are significantly homologous with E-values below $10 \times 10^{-30}$ to the 20 genes already known. The difference in gene numbers is due to the existence of homologous genes within the *A. oryzae* genome. For example, *A. oryzae* has five and three homologs of *S. cerevisiae* Rdr1p<sup>46)</sup> and Gal4p respectively, and three and three homologs of *A. nidulans* UaY<sup>47)</sup> and NirA<sup>48)</sup> respectively. These homologs must have emerged by gene duplication, but it is not known whether they have overlapping functions. Consequently, we can refer reliably to the physiological functions of only 19 *A. oryzae* Zn(II)<sub>2</sub>Cys<sub>6</sub> factors out of the 187 (Table 3); seven factors have been cloned from *A. oryzae*, and the other 12 factors have an obvious ortholog each characterized in other fungi. Most of these regulate carbon or nitrogen metabolism, except that PRO1 and RosA are involved in sexual development,<sup>49,50)</sup> and Rdr1p is a repressor of drug-resistance genes.<sup>46)</sup>

AmyR is a transcriptional activator for amylolytic genes in *Aspergillus*.<sup>28,51,52)</sup> AmyR-mediated transcription is controlled by regulation of its nuclear localization (Kobayashi, unpublished) in response to the physiological inducer isomaltose in *A. nidulans*.<sup>53)</sup> The *amyR* gene is clustered with *agdA* and *amyA* genes encoding $\alpha$-glucosidase and $\alpha$-amylase in both *A. oryzae* and *A. nidulans*. Deletion of the *amyR* gene causes significant defects in growth on both maltose and starch in *A. nidulans*,<sup>52)</sup> but only on starch in *A. oryzae* (Gomi, unpublished), suggesting that the genes under the control of AmyR differ slightly in *A. nidulans* and *A. oryzae*. Probably, *A. oryzae* has an $\alpha$-glucosidase(s) regulated by a transcription factor other than AmyR. A candidate for such a transcription factor is MalR, the gene for which forms a cluster similar to the *S. cerevisiae MAL* cluster<sup>54)</sup> with an $\alpha$-glucosidase and a maltose permease gene (Gomi, unpublished). *A. nidulans* possesses an orthologous cluster but, interestingly, expression of the $\alpha$-glucosidase gene in the cluster is AmyR-dependent (Kobayashi, unpublished). ## IV. Cell Signaling

### 4. Other transcription factors

Living cells, including fungi, are equipped with mechanisms for sensing various environmental stimuli, such as osmotic stress, oxidative stress, and pheromones, which allow adaptation to stimuli through a variety of cellular responses triggered by the sensing and subsequent signaling systems. Filamentous fungi, including the three *Aspergillus* species *A. oryzae*, *A. nidulans*, and *A. fumigatus*, are more tolerant to various conditions than yeasts, and hence they are expected to possess extensive networks for sensing and signaling. Analysis of genome information on the three *Aspergillus* species indicates that the organization of the sensing and signal transduction systems, and in particular of the upstream signaling machinery (G-protein coupled receptors, G-protein $\alpha$ subunits G$\alpha$, two-component signaling proteins, etc.) is more complex than that found in yeasts (Fig. 7). # 1. Two-component signaling

Two-component signaling genes. Two-component systems (or His-to-Asp phosphorelays), first described in bacteria, are a widely spread signal-transduction mechanism in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes. The systems consist of three types of common signal transducers: a sensor with histidine kinase activity, a response regulator containing a phospho-accepting receiver, and a histidine-containing phospho-transmitter (HPt). In addition to precisely examined bacterial systems, two-component systems are also well-characterized in eukaryotic microorganisms such as *S. cerevisiae* and *Shizosaccharomyces pombe*. These organisms have only a small number of common signal transducers as compared to prokaryotes: one histidine kinase, two response regulators, and one HPt in *S. cerevisiae*, but three histidine kinases, two response regulators, and one HPt in *S. pombe*. The yeast systems are involved in osmotic and oxidative stress responses, and also in regulation of the mitotic cell cycle and sexual development. In contrast to the yeast systems, a filamentous fungus, *N. crassa*, possesses more divergent signaling pathways, including 11 histidine kinases, two response regulators, and one Hpt.<sup>1)</sup> Genome analysis of the three *Aspergillus* species has revealed that they possess more redundant histidine kinases than *N. crassa* (Table 6). This divergence suggests the importance of the two component systems for the three *Aspergillus* species in responding to environmental stimuli.

Histidine kinases in Aspergillus. Two histidine kinase genes, *tcsA* and *tcsB*, have been reported in *A. nidulans*.<sup>95,96)</sup> The *tcsA* gene is required for sporulation under normal osmotic conditions, while the *tcsB* gene is a homolog of *S. cerevisiae SLN1*. The *FOS-1* gene of *A. fumigatus*, involved in cell-wall assembly, is the ortholog of *tcsA*.<sup>97)</sup> In addition to the *tcsA* and *tcsB* genes, *A. nidulans* possesses 13 putative histidine kinase genes, while *A. oryzae* and *A. fumigatus* possess 13 and 11 histidine kinase genes respectively, in addition to the *tcsA* and *tcsB* orthologs (Table 6). Based on amino acid sequence similarity, *Aspergillus* histidine kinases can be classified into nine families (HK1–9). Except for HK6 and HK8, each family is simply composed of orthologs. No orthologs or paralogs that correspond to *N. crassa* NCU02057.1, NCU09520.1, NCU03164.1, or NCU00939.1 are present in the three *Aspergillus* species.

The histidine kinase genes of the plant pathogens *Cochliobolus heterostrophus*, *Gibberella moniliformis*, and *Botryotinia fuckeliana* have been classified into 11 groups.<sup>98)</sup> Group I, consisting of *N. crassa* NCU09520 and its paralogs, and group XI, consisting of NCU00939.1, NCU03164.1, and their paralogs, are highly divergent within each group. Interestingly, the three *Aspergillus* species do not possess any histidine kinases classified into these groups, supporting the suggestion by Catlett *et al.*<sup>98)</sup> that these groups contain paralogs required for virulence. In addition, group II histidine kinases are absent in the *Aspergillus* species. In contrast, HK8 histidine kinases are specific to the *Aspergillus* species. Family HK8 is highly divergent internally, and contains seven, five, and five histidine kinases of *A. nidulans*, *A. oryzae*, and *A. fumigatus* respectively. Families HK1, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 are conserved among *Aspergillus*, *Neurospora*, and the plant pathogens.

Possible roles of Aspergillus histidine kinases. Family HK1 is composed of orthologs of *A. nidulans tcsB*. TcsB is a possible transmembrane osmosensor histidine kinase, because it is highly homologous to the osmosensor Sln1p of *S. cerevisiae*, and overexpression of *tcsB* cDNA can suppress the lethality of a temperature-sensitive osmosensing-defective *sln1-ts* mutant.<sup>95)</sup> *A. nidulans* and other *Aspergillus* species might have more complex osmoregulatory systems than *S. cerevisiae*, since the *A. nidulans tcsB* disruptant does not exhibit any detectable phenotypic defects on standard and stress media.

Family HK3 contains *A. nidulans tcsA* and *A. fumigatus FOS-1*. The *tcsA* disruptant does not produce conidia on standard *Aspergillus* media, but this defect is suppressed by growth on 1 M sorbitol.<sup>96)</sup> TcsA regulates nuclear localization of DevR, a transcription factor with the HLH motif involved in the development of *A. nidulans*.<sup>91)</sup> The *FOS-1* disruptant does not exhibit a detectable defect in either hyphal growth or morphology on solid media,<sup>97)</sup> but conidiophore development is delayed in liquid media as compared to the wild type. A possible role of FOS-1p in the regulation of cell-wall assembly has been suggested, since the disruptant is highly resistant to dicarboximide fungicides and Novozym 234.

## 2. MAP kinase cascades

*MAP kinase pathways.* Mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinase pathways are highly conserved signaling units found in eukaryotes, where they play essential roles in the response to environmental signals and hormones, growth factors, and cytokines. MAP kinase pathways control cell growth, morphogenesis, proliferation, and stress responses, and they are involved in many disease processes.<sup>103)</sup>

Central to each MAP kinase pathway are three tiers of protein kinases, a MAP kinase (MAPK), a MAP kinase kinase (MAPKK) or MEK, and a MAP kinase kinase kinase (MAPKKK) or MEKK (Fig. 8). MAPKKK phosphorylates and thereby activates MAPKK on serine and threonine within a conserved part at the N-terminal lobe of the kinase domain. Subsequently, MAPKK phosphorylates the MAP kinase on a threonine (sometimes serine) and tyrosine residue, which are located adjacent to each other, separated by a single amino acid (T/S-X-Y). The phosphorylation site is located in the activation loop of the catalytic domain; dual phosphorylation on threonine and tyrosine is necessary for activation of the MAP kinase. Typically, phosphorylation stimulates transfer of the MAP kinase from the cytosol to the nucleus, where it phosphorylates targets on serine/threonine followed by a proline. A portion of activated MAP kinase is apparently also present in the cytoplasm to mediate posttranslational effects.

MAPKKKs consist of an N-terminal regulatory and a C-terminal catalytic kinase domain. The regulatory domain locks the C-terminal kinase domain in the inactive state. Activation can occur by phosphorylation through an upstream protein kinase or through interaction with other proteins, a process that often involves small G-proteins. The activation mechanisms and sensor systems upstream of the MAP kinase pathways are diverse, and include receptor-tyrosine kinase (in animal systems), G-protein coupled receptors, phosphorelay systems, and so on.

Different MAP kinase pathways form interacting signaling systems. For instance, one MAPKK may control several different MAP kinases, as is observed even in the relatively simple yeast system. Different pathways within the same organism often share kinases. Especially in higher eukaryotes, but even in *S. cerevisiae*, this situation results in highly complex network systems of signaling pathways. Pathway specificity is commonly, but probably not exclusively, achieved by scaffold proteins. Scaffold proteins may be apparently dedicated to this purpose, such as *S. cerevisiae* Ste5p, or may form part of the active components of the MAP kinase cascade, such as yeast MAPKK Pbs2p.

integrity is known as calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein phosphatase, calcineurin, dependent signaling.<sup>133)</sup> The role of calcineurin (Cna1/2p-Cnb1p) is mediated by the Crz1p zinc finger-containing transcription factor, which is dephosphorylated by calcineurin, and consequently binds to a promoter element called the calcineurin-dependent response element (CDRE). The *FKS2* gene, which encodes an alternative subunit of $\beta$-1,3-glucan synthase in addition to Fks1p, is a known target of Crz1p.<sup>134)</sup>

In filamentous fungi, including *A. nidulans* and *Magnaporthe grisea*, several genes encoding MAP kinases have been isolated.<sup>112,135)</sup> Deletion analysis of *A. nidulans mpkA*, a counterpart of yeast *MPK1* (*SLT2*), suggests that the kinase is involved in the germination of conidial spores and in polarized growth.<sup>112)</sup> However, no transcription factor that is directly involved in cell wall biogenesis has yet been isolated or characterized in filamentous fungi. The gene homologs involved in cell wall biogenesis have been identified *in silico* based on the three *Aspergillus* genome data sets (Fig. 8). Most homologs to those involved in yeast cell wall biogenesis are well conserved in the three fungi. It is noteworthy that the *fksA* gene coding for $\beta$-1,3-glucan synthase, and the *cnaA* gene coding for calcineurin, are not redundant genes in the three fungi, and that most cell wall biogenesis genes that include signaling components are also non-redundant. *In silico* assignment of transcription factors is generally difficult because conserved regions in transcription factors are often restricted to DNA binding domains such as a zinc finger domain and a leucine zipper motif. Other motifs are generally unavailable to distinguish transcription factors from each other. Fujioka et al. isolated the *A. nidulans rlmA* gene, which encodes a transcription factor with high similarity to yeast Rlm1p, using Rlm1p-specific signatures such as MADS (MCM1, Agamous, Deficients, SRF) box and an Mpk1p-docking region.<sup>113)</sup>

In *A. oryzae*, the cell integrity pathway has been analyzed using a morphologic mutant in which the *kexB* gene, encoding a subtilisin-like processing enzyme similar to *S. cerevisiae* Kex2p, was disrupted.<sup>113)</sup> The *kexB* disruptant ($\Delta$*kexB*) formed shrunken colonies with poor conidia generation on Czapek-Dox (CD) agar plates and hyperbranched mycelia in CD liquid medium. The phenotypes of the $\Delta$*kexB* strain were restored under high osmolarity under both solid and liquid culture conditions. Transcription of the *mpkA* gene, which encodes a putative mitogen-activated protein kinase involved in cell integrity signaling, was significantly higher in $\Delta$*kexB* cells than in wild-type cells. The $\Delta$*kexB* cells also contained higher levels of transcripts for cell wall-related genes encoding $\beta$-1,3-glucanosyltransferase and chitin synthases. This is presumably attributable to cell integrity signaling through the increased gene expression of *mpkA*. Constitutively increased levels of phosphorylated MpkA were also observed in $\Delta$*kexB* cells on the CD plate culture. High

osmotic stress remarkably downregulated the increased levels both of transcripts of *mpkA* gene and of the phosphorylated form of MpkA protein in $\Delta$*kexB*, concomitantly suppressing the morphological defects. These results suggest that the upregulation of transcription levels of *mpkA* and cell wall biogenesis genes in the $\Delta$*kexB* strain is autoregulated by phosphorylated MpkA as the activated form through cell integrity signaling. Taking this together, it is predicted that KexB is required for precise proteolytic processing of sensor proteins in the cell integrity pathway or of cell wall-related enzymes under transcriptional control by the pathway. Thus the KexB defect induces disordered cell integrity signaling.

Since analysis of the *A. oryzae* genome information indicates that some genes coding for signaling proteins upstream (histidine kinases [HK6, HK8], GPCRs, G$\alpha$ proteins) and downstream (5 MAPKs) are redundant, functional studies of the genes are necessary to determine how signaling systems are organized in the fungus.

# V. Protein Secretion

Among the variety of filamentous fungi, the importance of *Aspergillus* species for commercial use has recently increased, especially with regard to *A. oryzae*.<sup>136)</sup> Owing to its ability to secrete a wide variety of useful proteins and enzymes, it is also being manipulated genetically to enhance its heterologous protein production abilities.<sup>137)</sup> The fact that *A. oryzae* secretes a variety of proteins and enzymes in solid state fermentation<sup>138)</sup> makes it a very useful host in industrial applications. Hence an understanding of the genes responsible for the protein export mechanism is indispensable.

Proteins destined for secretion are essentially synthesized in the cytosol and must pass through membranes and organelles (such as the endoplasmic reticulum, ER, Golgi, etc.,) before being processed, and secreted into the exterior.<sup>139)</sup> The general mechanism attributed to this process involves coordination of a proteinaceous translocation network apparatus. However, our knowledge of the principles of the protein secretory machinery in filamentous fungi is still emerging, and thus several of the essential players involved in this complex pathway are as yet unknown. Obviously, one of the main challenges in research on protein secretion in filamentous fungi is critically to understand protein translocation at a molecular level, and hence genomics at this juncture might certainly be a powerful tool to reveal distinct clues towards deepening our understanding of the various elements of this protein export network. The recent completion of the *A. oryzae* genome sequencing project is expected to improve our knowledge of this extremely useful fungus and to aid in attempts to devise strategies to utilize it more efficiently in the production of proteins and enzymes.

The major hurdle underlying heterologous protein production and secretion in filamentous fungi is protein quality and turnover. Although efficient processing and folding of heterologous proteins is a primary role of the secretory system, misfolding of the proteins causes innate stress which in turn activates a process called the unfolded protein response (UPR) to maintain internal homeostasis.<sup>140)</sup> While some of the genes encoding proteins responsible for the translocation and processing of synthesized proteins in the ER, such as the foldases and chaperones, have been characterized in filamentous fungi, there appears to be a lacuna in terms of understanding the reasons behind the unimproved heterologous protein production system in some fungi<sup>139)</sup> even after overexpression of some of the components involved in proper protein maturation. The protein secretion pathway is therefore bound to be complex and may involve many additional components that make filamentous fungi more prolific secretors than the yeasts.

The protein secretory pathway in eukaryotes consists of several distinct organelles through which the trafficking of proteins occurs, and for this purpose, it is essential that these organelles possess a proper docking and fusion apparatus, which permits specific recognition of the approaching vesicles. It is well established that the transport of proteins is mediated by coated vesicles that bud off from membrane-bound compartments and are subsequently targeted to appropriate organelles through fusion events. Therefore, a highly specific and distinct combination of sensing factors facilitates ordered membrane targeting and fusion (e.g., ER to Golgi or Golgi to vacuole or Golgi to cell membranes, etc.). Several of these factors are collectively called SNARE regulators. They participate combinatorially in protein trafficking events.<sup>141)</sup> SNAREs (Soluble *N*-ethylmaleimide-sensitive fusion protein Attachment protein REceptors) are conserved membrane proteins present on vesicles (v-SNAREs) and target membranes (t-SNAREs). By interacting between each other they form the core of the vesicular targeting process and function in intracellular membrane fusion events. In general, SNARE

proteins consist of an alpha helical SNARE domain, which assembles with the other compatible domains (or factors including the Rab GTPases, AAA-type ATPases, and other tethering complexes) and provides the driving force for bilayer fusion. Helices of the SNARE domain can be divided into four classes: the Qa (syntaxin) class, the Qb (Bos1) class, the Qc (Bet1) class, and R-SNAREs.<sup>142)</sup> In filamentous fungi, SNAREs have important roles in hyphal tip growth and the secretion of extracellular enzymes. Gupta and Heath have reported SNAREs from genomes of filamentous fungi, including *N. crassa* and *A. fumigatus*.<sup>143)</sup> A comparative account of SNAREs in *A. oryzae* and other *Aspergillus* species, in addition to an overview of other important genes participating in the protein processing in *A. oryzae*, is presented.

## 2. A comparative account of SNAREs in Aspergillus species

Most of the genes encoding SNAREs are equally distributed in the three Aspergilli and the sequences of SNARE domains are highly conserved, as in *N. crassa*.<sup>143)</sup> A list of the orthologs of SNAREs present in the three Aspergilli is shown in Table 9. No orthologs of Vam3 were found in the three Aspergilli genomes, confirming that it is specific to *S. cerevisiae* and *Candida albicans*.<sup>143)</sup> A single ortholog of *S. cerevisiae* Snc1 and Snc2, which are functionally redundant, was found in each Aspergillus. Another duplicated pair of SNAREs, Sso1 and Sso2, had a single counterpart in each Aspergillus. Interestingly, *A. oryzae* has an additional SNARE (AO070329000134), in which the SNARE domain shares homology with those of Sso SNAREs on plasma membranes, but the absolutely conserved glutamine residue in the central layer was replaced by histidine. Although according to the EST data, this gene (AO070329000134) was expressed in *A. oryzae* (AO1EST_SNGL0153, 1068) and also in *A. flavus* (TC5840), it is not yet clear whether this additional SNARE functions in *A. oryzae*. Other fungi such as *Magnaporthe grisea*, *Fusarium graminearum*,

*Cryptococcus neoformans*, and *Ustilago maydis* appear to contain two orthologs for Sso SNAREs with conserved glutamines. *N. crassa* also contains two Sso orthologs, NcSyn1 and NcSyn2.<sup>143)</sup> Both are involved in vegetative growth, but NcSyn2 perhaps has distinct roles in ascospore formation.<sup>145)</sup> *S. cerevisiae* Sso1, and not Sso2, is also required during sporulation,<sup>146)</sup> in conjunction with the sporulation-specific SNARE Spo20.<sup>147)</sup> Although there are no orthologs of the sporulation-specific SNARE Spo20, in all three Aspergilli and *N. crassa*, the role of Nsyn2 in ascospore formation hence indicates that the sporulation-specific SNARE complex is conserved in the fungal kingdom. The existence of an additional putative SNARE in *A. oryzae* suggests another exocytotic mechanism for the specific vesicle fusion step or, more likely, other (sexual) developmental stages in *A. oryzae*, although its sexual cycle has not yet been recognized. While the existence of almost all the homologs of genes encoding components of the protein secretion pathways of yeast in this group of filamentous fungi indicates a similar mechanism of secretion in operation, it is quite possible that filamentous fungi have additional components that enhance their protein production and secretion capabilities. The major difference is with reference to the polarized hyphal growth observed in fungi, which might be one factor contributing to higher potential for protein secretion.<sup>148)</sup> In general, it is thought that localization of SNAREs is stringently regulated in a specific and ordered fashion at each step of the secretory pathway. Hence a spatial distribution analysis of all the SNAREs in *A. oryzae* should not only elucidate the exact mechanism of establishing hyphal polarity via the secretory pathway, but should also help in the efficient use of this fungus in the production of important metabolites.

## VI. Conclusion

The genome sequence of *A. oryzae* has revealed striking metabolic diversity. This was overlooked for a long time perhaps because *A. oryzae* has been used mainly as a strong producer of amylases and proteases. The diversity obviously indicates the potential of the organism for further biotechnological applications as a source of many industrial enzymes other than amylases and proteases, as well as a number of primary and even secondary metabolites. It is a treasure house of enzymes and metabolites, and so the genome data should accelerate industrial application of the organism. *A. oryzae* has various beneficial characteristics for industrial application. It is a safe organism, and it secretes significant amounts of proteins, including heterologous ones. A great deal of information on *A. oryzae* cultures has been accumulated during the long history of industrial use of it in Japan, and a number of factories already exist to grow large masses of the organism.
